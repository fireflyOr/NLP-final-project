\title{What Does the Target Leave Behind?\\Sparse-Autoencoder Concept Pruning for Text--Tabular Learning}

\author{Or Davidovich\\Technion -- Israel Institute of Technology}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In text--tabular learning, each free-text column is embedded and compressed before a tree model sees it. Joint TAR
fine-tunes the encoder on the target so that this compression keeps target-relevant variation. We ask whether one can
go further by \emph{removing} concepts: we decompose each column's embeddings with a sparse autoencoder (SAE), test
every concept against a shuffled copy of itself inside one model with the table and all other concepts, subtract the
concepts that fail, and compress what remains. On the 20 text datasets of MulTaBench, pruning removes about 70\% of
the concepts (38\% of the embedding variance) while keeping 80\% of TAR's gain over frozen embeddings, but it does not
improve on Joint TAR: it is slightly but consistently worse ($-0.0035$ AUROC/$R^2$, 4/20 datasets, $p=0.006$).
Choosing the amount by nested cross-fitting, so that every decision is made on rows the encoder never saw, lets the
method opt out where removal hurts but yields no gain. \TBD{Against random removal of the same variance: \ldots}
We then test, in one paired run with three learners and multiplicity correction, whether the target helps more when
it reweights or adds information instead of deleting it (PLS instead of PCA, no compression, stacking a text model's
out-of-fold predictions, late fusion). \TBD{Result.}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Many real tables mix numbers and categories with free text: job ads, product reviews, wine descriptions. A standard
recipe embeds each text cell with a pretrained encoder, reduces it to a few dimensions, and gives the result to a
gradient-boosted tree model alongside the other columns \citep{shi2021multimodal,grinsztajn2022tree}. MulTaBench
\citep{multabench} evaluates two variants of this recipe. \emph{Joint Frozen} embeds with a frozen E5 encoder
\citep{wang2022e5} and keeps 30 principal components per column; \emph{Joint TAR} first fine-tunes the encoder on
the target, so that the principal components follow the variation that matters for the label.

Both variants keep \emph{everything} the text expresses and let compression decide what survives. A product
description, however, talks about many things---materials, shipping, brand tone---of which only a few relate to the
price. Principal components follow variance, not relevance, so a frequent but irrelevant theme can occupy components
that a weaker relevant theme needs. This suggests an explicit alternative: break the embedding into interpretable
concepts, remove those unrelated to the target, and only then compress.

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) provide such a decomposition: they write an embedding as a sparse, non-negative
combination of learned directions that tend to be individually interpretable
\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse,gao2024scaling}. We use them as a filter rather than as
features. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A concept-pruning layer for text--tabular pipelines: per-column SAE, joint sibling-aware relevance testing
        against shuffled ``shadow'' concepts, and subtractive map-back into the embedding space (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A leakage-controlled evaluation on all 20 MulTaBench text datasets, with a random-removal control and a
        nested cross-fitted variant that makes every pruning decision on rows the encoder never saw
        (\S\ref{sec:setup}).
  \item A negative but informative result: removing concepts does not beat target-aware fine-tuning, even with
        clean decisions (\S\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A paired comparison of other ways to use the target on top of TAR---supervised projection, no compression,
        stacking, late fusion---with LightGBM, tuned LightGBM and CatBoost, and Holm-corrected tests.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Text in tabular learning.} Benchmarks of tables with text fields \citep{shi2021multimodal,multabench}
show that tree ensembles over compressed text embeddings are strong baselines. Target-aware text representations
appear both in fine-tuning the encoder before compression (Joint TAR, \citealp{multabench}) and in tabular foundation
models that attend from target tokens to verbalised cells, such as TabSTAR \citep{arazi2025tabstar}.

\paragraph{Supervised dimensionality reduction.} Supervised PCA keeps only features correlated with the outcome
before extracting components \citep{bair2006prediction}; partial least squares finds directions of maximal
covariance with the target \citep{wold2001pls}. Our method applies the screening idea in an SAE concept basis instead
of raw coordinates, and removes concepts instead of selecting coordinates.

\paragraph{Feature relevance with correlated features.} Univariate screening and marginal importance are unreliable
when features are redundant \citep{strobl2008conditional}. Boruta \citep{kursa2010boruta} compares each feature
with permuted ``shadow'' copies inside one model; we extend it to groups of sibling SAE concepts. Out-of-fold
estimation and cross-fitting \citep{wolpert1992stacked,chernozhukov2018double} motivate our nested variant.

\paragraph{Sparse autoencoders.} SAEs are a central tool of mechanistic interpretability
\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse}; we use TopK SAEs \citep{gao2024scaling}. Prior work uses
SAE concepts to interpret or steer models; we test whether they can \emph{filter} representations for a downstream
predictor.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Pipeline.} For each text column $c$, let $x\in\mathbb{R}^{384}$ be the (frozen or TAR-tuned) E5
embedding of the string ``\texttt{passage: $c$: value}''. Joint Frozen and Joint TAR fit PCA-30 on the training
rows' $x$ and pass the 30 components to LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm} together with the table. We insert one step
before PCA, fitted on the training rows only.

\paragraph{Concept decomposition.} We train a TopK SAE \citep{gao2024scaling} with $L=2048$ latents and $k=32$ active
per input on the column's training embeddings: $z=\mathrm{TopK}(W_e(x-b))$, $\hat x = b + \sum_i z_i d_i$ with
unit-norm decoder directions $d_i$. The SAE is unsupervised. Concepts active on fewer than 20 training rows are never
tested and are always kept.

\paragraph{Joint, sibling-aware relevance.} Testing concepts one at a time fails in two ways: near-duplicate
``sibling'' concepts (common in large SAEs) each look dispensable because the other covers for it, and concepts that
matter only together with the table or other concepts look irrelevant. We therefore (i) cluster eligible concepts by
decoder direction (average-linkage cosine distance $<0.5$) into sibling groups; (ii) fit one LightGBM on
\emph{[table, all concepts, a shadow copy of every group]}, where a group's shadow is its activations with rows
permuted jointly; and (iii) drop a group whose total split gain does not exceed its own shadow's in a majority of
three repeats.

\paragraph{Map-back.} Dropped concepts are subtracted, keeping the SAE's reconstruction error:
$\tilde x = x-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}} z_i d_i$. PCA-30 is then fitted on $\tilde x$, so the downstream model sees
exactly as many text features as in Joint Frozen/TAR.

\paragraph{Choosing how much to remove.} The rule above fixes the amount implicitly. Two variants choose it from
data. \emph{Held-out}: rank groups by harm (variance taken divided by importance measured as the rise in loss when the
group is permuted on TAR's early-stopping rows) and pick the removal fraction $f\in\{0,.1,.25,.5,.75\}$ by loss on
those rows. \emph{Cross-fitted}: split the training rows into three parts; for each part, fine-tune TAR on the other
two, rank concepts there (variance taken divided by shadow-corrected gain, from one shadow-test LightGBM), and
evaluate every $f$ on the unseen part with a LightGBM on the same inputs as the final model (table and all text
columns). The $f$ with the lowest total held-out loss is applied to the fold's ordinary TAR. In both variants
$f=0$ (plain TAR) is a candidate, and a larger removal must lower the loss by more than 0.1\%.

\paragraph{Alternatives to removal.} On the same TAR embeddings we also test methods that use the target without
deleting anything: \emph{PLS-30} in place of PCA-30 \citep{wold2001pls}; \emph{no compression} (all 384 dimensions);
\emph{stacking}, which adds the out-of-fold predictions of a text-only MLP on frozen embeddings as extra columns
\citep{wolpert1992stacked}; and \emph{late fusion}, a logistic/linear meta-learner over the out-of-fold outputs of a
table-only LightGBM and the same text model.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data and protocol.} All 20 text datasets of MulTaBench (3 binary, 7 multiclass, 10 regression), with the
benchmark's splits (two repeats of 3-fold cross-validation; training sets capped at 10k rows) and metrics: AUROC
(one-vs-rest macro for multiclass) and $R^2$. Joint TAR follows the benchmark recipe: LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} of rank
16 on the last three E5 layers, a linear head, regression targets binned into 20 quantiles, early stopping on 10\% of
the training rows (at most 1{,}000). Within a fold, all TAR-based arms use the \emph{same} fine-tuned encoder; in the
final run its LoRA adapters are saved and restored for every arm and learner, so differences isolate what follows
the encoder.

\paragraph{Runs and learners.} The \emph{main run} evaluates pruning and frozen-embedding variants with LightGBM at
default settings, as in the benchmark. The \emph{final run} evaluates all TAR-based arms together with three
learners: default LightGBM; LightGBM tuned on its validation split (learning rate, depth, column subsampling;
12 configurations with early stopping); and CatBoost with the benchmark's defaults. The cross-fitted variant was run
on five datasets.

\paragraph{Controls.} Random pruning removes random eligible concepts from the same SAE, either the same
\emph{number} as \ours{} or, because the selected concepts are mostly weak ones, the same \emph{variance}.

\paragraph{Leakage control.} Every component that uses the target is fitted on the outer fold's training rows only;
test rows are never used. Out-of-fold folds keep rows with identical text together.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Methods that use the target are compared with our own Joint TAR on the same splits and
learner, using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over per-dataset means (folds of a dataset are not independent
and only reported as win/loss counts). Since several arms are compared with TAR, we report Holm-adjusted $p$-values.

\paragraph{Removal does not beat TAR.} In the main run, \ours{} on top of TAR is slightly but consistently worse than
TAR ($-0.0035$, median $-0.0023$; better on 4/20 datasets, $p=0.006$), across task types (binary $-0.0018$,
multiclass $-0.0012$, regression $-0.0057$); the final run gives \TBD{$\Delta$, $p$}. Choosing the amount on TAR's
early-stopping rows was worse still on the six datasets where it ran (17 of 23 folds lost): on about a thousand
rows, the candidate amounts differ by less than the selection noise. Per-dataset scores are in Appendix~\ref{app:main}.

\paragraph{How much is removed, and is it the right part?} About 70\% of the eligible concepts, carrying 38\% of the
variance of TAR embeddings (the SAE leaves 2\% unexplained), at a cost of $0.0035$; \ours{} keeps 80\% of TAR's gain
over Joint Frozen. Removing the same \emph{number} of random concepts is much worse ($+0.0174$ for \ours{}, 18/20),
but this control removes 57\% of the variance, so it overstates the selection's value. Against random removal of the
same variance (Table~\ref{tab:removal}), \TBD{result}. \TBD{n\%} of the removed groups were never split on, either by
the concept or by its shadow, so ``removed'' partly means ``unused'' rather than ``shown irrelevant''. Sibling grouping
rarely merged concepts (1338 groups for 1348 eligible concepts); redundant concepts inside the joint model share gain
rather than being tested as a unit, so the sibling problem is mitigated, not solved.

\paragraph{Clean decisions do not change the picture.} The cross-fitted variant (five datasets, Table~\ref{tab:xf}
in Appendix~\ref{app:main}) is on par with TAR ($-0.0006$). On Kickstarter and DS salary every removal raises the
held-out loss, so it chooses 0\% and reproduces TAR exactly; on Baby product prices it chooses moderate amounts and
gains slightly. On Fake job postings and Jigsaw it chooses 75\% in every fold, which lowers the inner models' held-out
log loss by 3--10\%, yet the final model is slightly worse than TAR: removal helped encoders fine-tuned on two thirds
of the rows, not the one fine-tuned on all of them.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

We used one encoder (E5-small) and one SAE size. A pilot with TabSTAR \citep{arazi2025tabstar} as the backbone was
infeasible on our hardware (two epochs within its 10-hour budget on one dataset) and is not reported. Cross-fitting
costs three extra fine-tunes per fold and was run on five datasets only. In three multiclass runs of the main run
(stacking on Spotify, two folds; held-out pruning on Wine, one fold) the final model collapsed to chance level (test
log loss $>29$); we have not identified the cause and report them unchanged.

\TBD{Conclusion, once the final run is in.} Concept pruning separates concepts the model uses from those it does not,
but after target-aware fine-tuning removing the rest does not improve prediction.

\title{What Does the Target Leave Behind?\\Sparse-Autoencoder Concept Pruning for Text--Tabular Learning}

\author{Or Davidovich\\Technion -- Israel Institute of Technology}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
In text--tabular learning, each free-text column is embedded and compressed before a tree model sees it. Joint TAR
fine-tunes the encoder on the target so that this compression keeps target-relevant variation. We ask whether one can
go further by \emph{removing} concepts: we decompose each column's embeddings with a sparse autoencoder (SAE), judge
every concept jointly with its sibling concepts and the table, subtract the concepts that do not help predict the
target, and compress what remains. Across the 20 text datasets of MulTaBench, the selection is far better than
removing the same number of random concepts ($+0.017$ AUROC/$R^2$, 18/20 datasets, $p<10^{-4}$), and 70\% of the
concepts (38\% of the embedding variance) can be removed while keeping 80\% of TAR's gain over frozen embeddings.
Yet removal does not improve on Joint TAR: it is slightly but consistently worse ($-0.0035$, 4/20 datasets,
$p=0.006$). Choosing the amount by nested cross-fitting, so that every decision is made on rows the encoder never
saw, makes the method opt out where removal hurts but still yields no gain. Our analysis suggests that after
target-aware fine-tuning little removable variance is left, and that supervision is better spent on adding
target-aware signal than on deleting concepts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Many real tables mix numbers and categories with free text: job ads, product reviews, wine descriptions. A standard
recipe embeds each text cell with a pretrained encoder, reduces it to a few dimensions, and gives the result to a
gradient-boosted tree model alongside the other columns \citep{shi2021multimodal,grinsztajn2022tree}. MulTaBench
\citep{multabench} evaluates two variants of this recipe. \emph{Joint Frozen} embeds with a frozen E5 encoder
\citep{wang2022e5} and keeps 30 principal components per column; \emph{Joint TAR} first fine-tunes the encoder on
the target, so that the principal components follow the variation that matters for the label.

Both variants keep \emph{everything} the text expresses and let compression decide what survives. A product
description, however, talks about many things---materials, shipping, brand tone---of which only a few relate to the
price. Principal components follow variance, not relevance, so a frequent but irrelevant theme can occupy components
that a weaker relevant theme needs. This suggests an explicit alternative: break the embedding into interpretable
concepts, remove those unrelated to the target, and only then compress.

Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) provide such a decomposition: they write an embedding as a sparse, non-negative
combination of learned directions that tend to be individually interpretable
\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse,gao2024scaling}. We use them as a filter rather than as
features. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A concept-pruning layer for text--tabular pipelines: per-column SAE, joint sibling-aware relevance testing
        against shuffled ``shadow'' concepts, and subtractive map-back into the embedding space (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A leakage-controlled evaluation on all 20 MulTaBench text datasets, with a random-removal control and a
        nested cross-fitted variant that makes every pruning decision on rows the encoder never saw
        (\S\ref{sec:setup}).
  \item A negative but informative result: the method knows \emph{which} concepts matter, but removing the rest
        does not beat target-aware fine-tuning; we analyse why (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Text in tabular learning.} Benchmarks of tables with text fields \citep{shi2021multimodal,multabench}
show that tree ensembles over compressed text embeddings are strong baselines. Target-aware text representations
appear both in fine-tuning the encoder before compression (Joint TAR, \citealp{multabench}) and in tabular foundation
models that attend from target tokens to verbalised cells, such as TabSTAR \citep{arazi2025tabstar}.

\paragraph{Supervised dimensionality reduction.} Supervised PCA keeps only features correlated with the outcome
before extracting components \citep{bair2006prediction}; partial least squares finds directions of maximal
covariance with the target \citep{wold2001pls}. Our method applies the screening idea in an SAE concept basis instead
of raw coordinates, and removes concepts instead of selecting coordinates.

\paragraph{Feature relevance with correlated features.} Univariate screening and marginal importance are unreliable
when features are redundant \citep{strobl2008conditional}. Boruta \citep{kursa2010boruta} compares each feature
with permuted ``shadow'' copies inside one model; we extend it to groups of sibling SAE concepts. Out-of-fold
estimation and cross-fitting \citep{wolpert1992stacked,chernozhukov2018double} motivate our nested variant.

\paragraph{Sparse autoencoders.} SAEs are a central tool of mechanistic interpretability
\citep{bricken2023monosemanticity,cunningham2023sparse}; we use TopK SAEs \citep{gao2024scaling}. Prior work uses
SAE concepts to interpret or steer models; we test whether they can \emph{filter} representations for a downstream
predictor.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Pipeline.} For each text column $c$, let $x\in\mathbb{R}^{384}$ be the (frozen or TAR-tuned) E5
embedding of the string ``\texttt{passage: $c$: value}''. Joint Frozen and Joint TAR fit PCA-30 on the training
rows' $x$ and pass the 30 components to LightGBM \citep{ke2017lightgbm} together with the table. We insert one step
before PCA, fitted on the training rows only.

\paragraph{Concept decomposition.} We train a TopK SAE \citep{gao2024scaling} with $L=2048$ latents and $k=32$ active
per input on the column's training embeddings: $z=\mathrm{TopK}(W_e(x-b))$, $\hat x = b + \sum_i z_i d_i$ with
unit-norm decoder directions $d_i$. The SAE is unsupervised. Concepts active on fewer than 20 training rows are never
tested and are always kept.

\paragraph{Joint, sibling-aware relevance.} Testing concepts one at a time fails in two ways: near-duplicate
``sibling'' concepts (common in large SAEs) each look dispensable because the other covers for it, and concepts that
matter only together with the table or other concepts look irrelevant. We therefore (i) cluster eligible concepts by
decoder direction (average-linkage cosine distance $<0.5$) into sibling groups; (ii) fit one LightGBM on
\emph{[table, all concepts, a shadow copy of every group]}, where a group's shadow is its activations with rows
permuted jointly; and (iii) drop a group whose total split gain does not exceed its own shadow's in a majority of
three repeats.

\paragraph{Map-back.} Dropped concepts are subtracted, keeping the SAE's reconstruction error:
$\tilde x = x-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}} z_i d_i$. PCA-30 is then fitted on $\tilde x$, so the downstream model sees
exactly as many text features as in Joint Frozen/TAR.

\paragraph{Choosing how much to remove.} The rule above fixes the amount implicitly. Two variants choose it from
data. \emph{Held-out}: rank groups by harm (variance taken divided by importance measured as the rise in loss when the
group is permuted on TAR's early-stopping rows) and pick the removal fraction $f\in\{0,.1,.25,.5,.75\}$ by loss on
those rows. \emph{Cross-fitted}: split the training rows into three parts; for each part, fine-tune TAR on the other
two, rank concepts there (variance taken divided by shadow-corrected gain, from one shadow-test LightGBM), and
evaluate every $f$ on the unseen part with a LightGBM on the same inputs as the final model (table and all text
columns). The $f$ with the lowest total held-out loss is applied to the fold's ordinary TAR. In both variants
$f=0$ (plain TAR) is a candidate, and a larger removal must lower the loss by more than 0.1\%.

\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data and protocol.} All 20 text datasets of MulTaBench (3 binary, 7 multiclass, 10 regression), using the
benchmark's own splits (two repeats of 3-fold cross-validation, 6 folds; training sets capped at 10k rows) and
metrics: AUROC (one-vs-rest macro for multiclass) and $R^2$. Joint TAR follows the benchmark recipe: LoRA
\citep{hu2022lora} of rank 16 on the last three E5 layers, a linear head, regression targets binned into 20
quantiles, early stopping on 10\% of the training rows. All arms share the fold's split, LightGBM configuration,
and---for TAR-based arms---the \emph{same} fine-tuned encoder, so differences isolate the pruning step.

\paragraph{Controls.} \emph{Random pruning} removes the same number of eligible concepts, chosen at random, from the
same SAE. We also report frozen-embedding variants (relevance pruning and \ours{} without fine-tuning), PLS-30
\citep{wold2001pls}, and out-of-fold stacking of a text-only model's predictions \citep{wolpert1992stacked}.

\paragraph{Leakage control.} Every component that uses the target (SAE ranking, shadow tests, amount selection,
stacking) is fitted on the training rows of the outer fold only; test rows are never used. Stacking folds keep rows
with identical text together, since duplicated texts otherwise leak through the out-of-fold models.

\paragraph{Statistics.} Because methods that use the target must be compared with Joint TAR, we test each arm against
our own TAR run on the same splits with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over per-dataset means; the 6 folds of a
dataset are not independent and are only reported as win/loss counts.

\paragraph{Removal does not beat TAR.} Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarises all arms against Joint TAR. Concept
pruning on top of TAR is slightly worse ($-0.0035$, median $-0.0023$; better on 4/20 datasets, $p=0.006$), across all
task types (binary $-0.0018$, multiclass $-0.0012$, regression $-0.0057$). Every frozen-embedding variant is below
TAR on average; only stacking is not significantly worse ($p=0.55$).
Choosing the amount on TAR's early-stopping rows was worse still on the six datasets where we ran it (17 of 23 folds
lost) and was stopped: on roughly a thousand rows, the candidate amounts differ by less than the selection noise.
Per-dataset scores are in Appendix~\ref{app:main}.

\paragraph{But the selection is far from random.} Removing the same number of random concepts from the same SAE is
much worse: \ours{} beats it by $+0.0174$ on 18/20 datasets ($p<10^{-4}$); the same holds on frozen embeddings
for univariate relevance pruning ($+0.0074$, 19/20). The test therefore reliably identifies which concepts matter.
Table~\ref{tab:removal} shows how much is removed: about 70\% of the eligible concepts, carrying 38\% of the variance
of TAR embeddings (the SAE leaves only 2\% unexplained). Removing that much costs only $0.0035$, i.e.\ \ours{} keeps
80\% of TAR's gain over Joint Frozen. Sibling grouping rarely merged concepts (1338 groups for 1348 eligible
concepts), so the joint LightGBM, not the clustering, carries the ``judged with its siblings'' property.

\paragraph{Why there is no headroom.} Three observations are consistent with one explanation. First, removal is
nearly free but never pays: 38\% of the variance goes for a $0.0035$ loss, so what is removed is mostly irrelevant,
but PCA-30 on TAR embeddings was not spending much capacity on it either. Second, the cross-fitted runs suggest that
the benefit shrinks as the encoder improves: removal helps the inner encoders, fine-tuned on two thirds of the rows,
but not the final encoder fine-tuned on all of them. (The inner criterion is log loss and the reported metric is
AUROC, which may also contribute.) Third, adding target-aware signal works where deleting does not: stacking a
text-only model's out-of-fold predictions onto frozen embeddings improves on Joint Frozen on 19/20 datasets
($+0.0086$, $p<0.001$) and is statistically indistinguishable from TAR ($p=0.55$), whereas \ours{} on frozen
embeddings is worse than Joint Frozen ($-0.0040$, 4/20). The supervision helps through what it adds or keeps, not
through what it deletes.

\section{Limitations and conclusion}

We used a single encoder (E5-small), one SAE size, and LightGBM as the only downstream model. A pilot with TabSTAR
\citep{arazi2025tabstar} as the backbone was infeasible on our hardware (two epochs within its 10-hour budget on one
dataset) and is not reported as a result. Held-out rows for choosing the removal amount are few; cross-fitting
addresses this at three times the fine-tuning cost and was run on five datasets only. In three multiclass runs
(stacking on Spotify, folds 1 and 5; held-out pruning on Wine, fold 1) the final model collapsed to chance level
(test log loss $>29$, AUROC near 0.5); we have not identified the cause and report them unchanged.

Concept pruning is a principled, interpretable filter that reliably separates relevant from irrelevant concepts, but
after target-aware fine-tuning it finds little left to remove. For practitioners, the lesson is to spend supervision
on \emph{what to keep or add}---fine-tuning or stacking a supervised text model---rather than on what to delete.
